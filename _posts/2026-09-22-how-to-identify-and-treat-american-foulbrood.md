American foulbrood is the disease every beekeeper dreads finding in the brood box. It moves quietly through a colony, spreads through shared equipment and robbing bees, and has no genuine cure once larvae are infected. The standard response - burning infected hives and burying the ash - sounds extreme the first time you read it. It is not an overreaction. The spore-forming bacterium responsible for AFB can remain viable in wood, wax, and honey for 35 to 80 years, making fire and burial the only reliable elimination method.

What makes AFB manageable is that it has clear, recognizable signs. A beekeeper who inspects regularly and knows what to look for can catch an infection before it spreads to neighboring colonies. This guide covers the visual indicators you need to know, the field tests that confirm suspicion, the legal steps most states require, and how to rebuild cleanly after a confirmed infection.

**One important note before you begin:** in most US states, American foulbrood is a reportable disease. If you suspect it, contact your state apiarist before taking any action. They can confirm the diagnosis at no cost and walk you through the protocol required in your jurisdiction.

## What Makes American Foulbrood Different from Other Brood Diseases

AFB is caused by the spore-forming bacterium *Paenibacillus larvae*. Spores of *P. larvae* are nearly indestructible under normal conditions, remaining viable in comb, wood, and honey for 35 to 80 years. A single infected larval cell contains millions of spores. When nurse bees attempt to clean out dead larvae - their normal hygienic behavior - they pick up spores on their bodies and spread them to more cells. Robbing bees carry spores out of the hive entirely, spreading infection to other colonies in your apiary and to neighboring apiaries downrange.

European foulbrood (EFB), caused by *Melissococcus plutonius*, presents very differently: larvae die before capping, appear yellow-brown and twisted rather than dark and ropy, and colonies sometimes recover on their own with a strong nectar flow or requeening. Sacbrood, caused by a virus, produces a scattered brood pattern but lacks AFB's characteristic smell or ropiness and typically resolves without beekeeper intervention. Neither disease leaves a spore load that survives in equipment for decades.

The practical consequence of AFB's spore biology is direct: you cannot medicate your way through an active infection. Antibiotics suppress the vegetative form of *P. larvae*—the active, dividing cells that kill larvae - but they do not penetrate spores embedded in cell walls. A colony that improves during a treatment window still harbors the full spore load in its comb, and the infection resurges when treatment ends.

AFB also spreads efficiently through beekeeper activity. Sharing frames between hives, moving honey supers without inspection, and accepting used equipment from unknown sources are all documented transmission routes. Keep tools apiary-specific and be selective about used equipment.

## How to Identify American Foulbrood in Your Hive

The best time to look for AFB is during late spring through midsummer, when brood production is at its peak. Work through the brood box methodically, holding each frame at an angle to the light so the cappings are clearly visible.

**Sunken and discolored cappings.** Healthy capped brood has slightly convex cappings in a uniform tan color. AFB cappings sink inward - forming a concave depression - and darken to brown or black. Many appear greasy or moist on the surface, and some have a small hole where bees tried and failed to remove the contents.

**Irregular brood pattern.** An infected frame shows a scattered mix of capped cells, empty cells, and uncapped larvae with no consistent block of solid brood. This "pepper pot" pattern also appears with varroa damage and failing queens, so treat it as a signal to inspect further rather than a standalone diagnosis.

**The smell.** AFB produces a distinctive odor that many beekeepers describe as rotting wood glue or putrefied flesh. If you detect an unusual smell mixed with the normal beeswax scent when you open the hive, inspect the brood frames carefully before continuing.

**Brown, liquified brood.** Open a suspicious capped cell with a [hive tool](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07M7VT8F1?tag=mbbeekeeping-20) or a thin stick. A healthy larva is white, plump, and shiny. An AFB-infected larva is a dark brown, coffee-colored mass that has partially melted into the lower cell wall.

**The ropiness test.** This is the most reliable field confirmation available without lab equipment. Insert a matchstick, wooden skewer, or bent piece of wire into a suspicious cell, press gently, and withdraw it slowly. AFB-positive material stretches into a thin, elastic rope of one to three inches before breaking. Sacbrood, EFB, and other diseases that produce discolored brood do not show this ropiness. A positive rope pull is strong presumptive evidence of AFB.

**Scale.** As the liquified larva dries, it leaves a hard, dark, boat-shaped crust - called scale - permanently bonded to the lower cell wall. AFB scale cannot be dislodged by scraping, unlike EFB scale. Hold the frame at a 45-degree angle toward a light source and look through the cells; scale appears as a dark crescent inside. A frame covered in scale represents a late-stage infection with an enormous spore load.

Not every early infection will show all six signs at once. Two or three signs, including a positive ropiness test, are sufficient grounds to quarantine the hive and contact your state apiarist before moving anything.

## Confirming an American Foulbrood Diagnosis

A strong field case - sunken cappings, the smell, and a positive ropiness test - is usually sufficient for an experienced state apiarist to make a presumptive diagnosis. Laboratory confirmation removes remaining doubt and is worth pursuing before you take irreversible action.

**State apiarist inspection.** Most US states provide free AFB inspections through their state apiarist program. Contact your state's department of agriculture to find the right office. In states where AFB is a reportable disease, this contact is legally required.

**Comb sample submission.** The [USDA AMS Honey Bee Health](https://www.ams.usda.gov/services/auditing/honey-bees) program provides guidance on submitting infected comb for diagnostic testing. Cut a section approximately 4 inches square, wrap it in dry newspaper - not plastic, which promotes mold - place it in a rigid container, and mail it to the appropriate diagnostic lab. Results typically return within one to two weeks.

**PCR testing.** Polymerase chain reaction testing detects *P. larvae* DNA directly and can identify spore presence before visible symptoms develop. Several university extension labs and commercial diagnostic services offer this option. It is particularly valuable when evaluating used equipment from an unknown source before integrating it into your apiary.

The rule for confirmation is straightforward: do not destroy equipment without a confirmed or strongly presumptive diagnosis, and do not move, combine, or reuse anything from a suspected hive until you have clarity.

## How to Respond When You Confirm American Foulbrood

> **Safety note:** Pit burning hive equipment involves open flame and hot ash. Follow all local open-burning regulations, maintain a water source on site, and never burn in dry or windy conditions. Ash and charred material from an AFB burn still contain viable spores - bury everything at least 18 inches deep and cover the site completely.

Once AFB is confirmed, response must follow a specific sequence. Acting out of order - especially moving frames before notifying your state apiarist - is the primary mechanism by which a single infected hive spreads to an entire apiary.

1. **Quarantine the hive immediately.** Close all entrances and mark the hive clearly. Do not reopen it until you are prepared to execute the full response. Wash gloves and all tools that contacted the infected equipment with a 10% bleach solution before working any other hive.

2. **Notify your state apiarist.** Contact your state's department of agriculture to report the suspected or confirmed infection. This is a legal requirement in most US states and provides you with official guidance on the protocol required in your jurisdiction.

3. **Confirm the diagnosis.** If you have not yet performed the ropiness test and received a presumptive or confirmed diagnosis, complete that step now. Do not proceed to destruction based on a single ambiguous visual sign.

4. **Destroy infected equipment according to your state's protocol.** The USDA and most state apiarists recommend a pit burn: dig a hole at the site, place the entire hive - bees, frames, comb, wooden boxes, and all porous components - into the pit and burn completely. Plan the burn for dusk or early morning when most foragers are inside the hive. Metal hardware such as screened bottom boards, queen excluders, and entrance reducers can be removed, scorched thoroughly with a propane torch, and retained for reuse.

5. **Bury the ash at least 18 inches deep.** Ash and charred wood from the burn still harbor viable spores. Cover the site completely to prevent access by foraging bees from neighboring colonies.

6. **Decontaminate all tools and protective gear.** Wash hive tools with a 10% bleach solution or 70% isopropyl alcohol. Launder your veil and suit.

7. **Inspect every other hive in the apiary.** Conduct a thorough brood inspection on all remaining colonies. If you recently moved frames or shared equipment between hives, extend the inspection to any colony that received those materials. Apply the ropiness test to any suspicious cell.

## Preventing American Foulbrood Before It Takes Hold

Prevention is more reliable than detection and far less costly than remediation. The spores of *P. larvae* are essentially omnipresent - in soil, old equipment, and potentially in commercial honey - so the goal is to minimize conditions that allow infection to establish and to maintain colonies strong enough to mount effective hygienic responses.

**Source bees carefully and request documentation.** Package bees and nucs from suppliers who document state apiary inspections and disease testing represent the lowest baseline risk. Ask whether the source apiary has been inspected by a state apiarist within the past year. Our guide on [How To Order Package Bees](/2026/04/14/how-to-order-package-bees/) covers the specific questions worth asking before committing to a supplier.

**Never feed bees honey of unknown origin.** This is the most common AFB introduction route in hobbyist apiaries. Commercial honey may contain viable AFB spores, and honey from other beekeepers whose hive health is unverified carries the same risk. Feed only sugar syrup or your own tested honey.

**Inspect brood at least twice per active season.** A thorough inspection in early spring and again in midsummer catches most infections before they reach a scale where spread is difficult to contain. Keep written inspection records so you can identify subtle brood-pattern changes over successive visits.

**Maintain hygienic stock.** Colonies bred for hygienic behavior - the ability to detect and remove diseased brood before it becomes a spore reservoir - show measurably better AFB resistance. The [University of Minnesota Hygienic Bee program](https://bees.umn.edu/minnesota-hygienic-line) has documented this trait across multiple breeding cycles. Sourcing from breeders who select for hygienic traits is one of the most defensible disease-prevention strategies available.

**Rotate old comb regularly.** Comb darkens and accumulates pathogens over time, with older frames representing a higher residual spore load. Most extension services recommend removing brood comb that is three to five years old as routine management.

**Approach antibiotic prophylaxis with caution.** Oxytetracycline — available in various Terramycin-branded veterinary products, including [this formulation](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00027DA94?tag=mbbeekeeping-20) — and tylosin tartrate suppress the vegetative form of *P. larvae* and may reduce the rate of new larval infections. They do not kill spores. In the United States, oxytetracycline for honey bee use requires a valid veterinarian-client-patient relationship (VCPR) and a prescription. Honey from antibiotic-treated colonies cannot legally be sold. Antibiotic prophylaxis is not a substitute for strong biosecurity practices.

## Rebuilding Your Apiary After an American Foulbrood Event

A confirmed AFB event followed by a complete burn-and-bury response gives you a genuinely clean starting point. The temptation to salvage materials is understandable - hive bodies and frames represent real money - but partial salvage frequently leads to partial failure. One contaminated frame restarts the cycle.

Wait a minimum of 30 days after the burn before installing new bees at the site. During that interval, assess any equipment you plan to retain. Metal components that were flame-scorched with a propane torch are generally safe for reuse. Any wooden equipment that held frames from the infected hive is high-risk regardless of visual appearance - replacement is the correct choice.

Keep detailed written records from that first inspection forward; knowing what a healthy brood pattern looks like in your new colony is the foundation of every early-detection call you will make going forward.

## Common Mistakes Beekeepers Make with American Foulbrood

Mishandling AFB typically falls into one of two patterns: overreacting to symptoms that turn out to be sacbrood, or underreacting to genuine signs until the infection is severe. These are the specific mistakes most likely to cost you colonies or equipment.

**Moving frames from a suspected hive before confirming the diagnosis.** The impulse to transfer a suspicious frame to another box for a better look is one of the fastest ways to spread AFB through an apiary. If you see sunken cappings or detect an unusual smell, stop your inspection, close the hive, and do not move anything until you have performed the ropiness test and contacted your state apiarist. A single contaminated frame is sufficient to establish infection in a healthy colony.

**Treating active AFB with antibiotics and expecting a cure.** Antibiotics suppress vegetative *P. larvae* but do not eliminate spores from existing scale. A colony that appears to recover during the treatment window still harbors a full spore reservoir in its comb. The infection resurges when treatment ends, and the contaminated comb continues to threaten every bee that contacts it. Relying on antibiotics to manage an active infection while delaying notification or required destruction increases the risk of spread.

**Leaving pit burn ash exposed on the surface.** Ash and charred wood from a complete hive burn remain spore-positive. An uncovered burn site invites foraging bees from nearby colonies to investigate, pick up spores, and carry them home. The 18-inch burial depth is not arbitrary — it places the material below the range that most surface-foraging bee behavior reaches.

**Accepting used equipment without screening it.** Hive bodies, frames, and supers from beekeepers who have retired or downsized carry an unpredictable disease risk. The absence of visible scale or odor does not guarantee freedom from viable AFB spores. Inspect every frame, perform the ropiness test on any suspicious residue, and consider PCR testing before the equipment contacts active colonies.

**Skipping the state apiarist notification.** The state apiarist is a resource, not an enforcement officer. Their mandate is to help beekeepers manage disease outbreaks, and reporting also protects other beekeepers in your area who share airspace with your apiary. ## Frequently Asked Questions About American Foulbrood

**Can you treat American foulbrood without burning the hive?**

In established infections, burning remains the only protocol that reliably eliminates the spore load from an apiary. Antibiotics suppress the vegetative bacteria that kill larvae but do not reach or neutralize spores embedded in cell walls and dried scale. A colony treated with antibiotics may appear to recover while still harboring scale-laden comb that re-infects the next generation of brood after treatment ends. Gamma irradiation of comb does kill AFB spores and has been used in some countries as an alternative to burning, but access to irradiation facilities is not practical for most hobbyist beekeepers in the United States. When a state apiarist recommends burning, that recommendation reflects decades of documented outcomes.

**How do I tell American foulbrood apart from sacbrood?**

The ropiness test is the clearest field differentiator. AFB-infected material, when probed with a thin stick and slowly withdrawn, stretches into a thin elastic rope of one to three inches before breaking. Sacbrood does not produce this ropiness - the material pulls out as a loose, watery mass. Sacbrood larvae also tend to die before capping, and capped cases show a distinctive shape where the head has turned upward inside the cell, giving a "gondola" appearance when the capping is removed. The odors differ as well: sacbrood has a faintly sour smell, while AFB produces the more pronounced putrefied or rotting-wood-glue odor. When the ropiness test is equivocal, request a state apiarist inspection rather than acting on ambiguous evidence.

**Is American foulbrood dangerous to humans or other animals?**

No. *Paenibacillus larvae* is host-specific to honey bee larvae and poses no known health risk to humans, livestock, other insects, or companion animals. The real hazard from handling AFB material is spreading spores to your healthy colonies or to neighboring apiaries. Use tools dedicated to the suspected hive, decontaminate them thoroughly before they contact any other equipment, and do not carry potentially contaminated frames, comb, or clothing into spaces where clean beekeeping materials are stored.

**Do I have to report American foulbrood to a state authority?**

In most US states, AFB is a reportable pest or disease under state apiary law, meaning beekeepers are legally obligated to notify the state apiarist or department of agriculture when they suspect or confirm an infection. Specific requirements - notification timeline, method, and mandated response - vary by state. Your state apiarist can confirm the diagnosis at no cost, clarify your legal obligations, walk you through the destruction protocol required in your jurisdiction, and help assess neighboring apiaries for potential spread. Contact information is available through your state's department of agriculture or through the [USDA APHIS Honey Bee Health](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/livestock-poultry-disease/bees) page.

American foulbrood is serious, but it is not the end of your beekeeping if you catch it early and respond correctly. The burn-and-rebuild protocol is painful the first time. It is far less painful than watching AFB move silently through an entire apiary because an early infection was misread or managed quietly with antibiotics. Your next colony, installed on clean equipment with documented healthy stock, has every reason to thrive.
